1. TITLE OF INVENTION

"A self-powered IoT-based in-tank water quality monitoring and automated sediment-flush system with edge-computed composite scoring, closed-loop feedback and local-network dashboard for domestic and institutional water storage tanks"

2. FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of Internet of Things (IoT) enabled water storage tank maintenance and water quality management. More particularly, the invention pertains to an integrated, self-powered IoT sensing, edge-computing and actuation unit that is removably installed within a domestic or institutional water storage tank (overhead or underground), continuously measures key water quality parameters of the stored water, computes at the network edge a composite water quality score indicative of accumulated contamination, and automatically or semi-automatically actuates a bottom-mounted flush valve to selectively remove the settled sediment and particulate layer at the tank floor — without draining the tank — when the computed quality score indicates that settled contamination has exceeded a predetermined threshold.

IoT Characterisation of the Invention: The invention is an edge-computing IoT system in which (i) a battery of physical sensors forms the perception layer, (ii) an on-device microcontroller forms the edge/fog computing layer where scoring, trend analysis and event classification are executed locally, (iii) a Wi-Fi access point + embedded web server forms the local communication layer serving an HTML dashboard to any browser on the LAN, and (iv) a valve actuator forms the physical actuation layer that closes the loop. No cloud, no subscription and no internet uplink is required for any core function — a deliberate architectural distinction over prior-art IoT water monitors.

3. BACKGROUND AND PROBLEM STATEMENT

3.1 The Forgotten Tank Problem

Water storage tanks in residential households, schools, hospitals, hostels, apartment complexes, and commercial buildings across India and similar tropical regions are among the most neglected components of the safe drinking water chain. Municipal or borewell water entering these tanks is typically treated and relatively clean at the point of entry. However, water that is stored in tanks for hours to weeks undergoes progressive degradation through the following mechanisms:

  1. Dissipation of disinfectant residual — Free chlorine, which inhibits microbial growth, dissipates within 24 to 48 hours of storage, removing the primary safeguard against bacterial proliferation.
  2. Sedimentation of suspended particles — Fine particulate matter (silt, clay, corrosion products, organic detritus) carried in by the source water settles under gravity onto the tank floor, forming a persistent sludge layer that is not removed by normal outlet flow.
  3. Biofilm formation — Microbial colonies colonize the submerged internal surfaces (walls, floor, baffles, inlet pipe exteriors), forming a slimy biofilm matrix that periodically sheds cells and fragments into the stored water.
  4. Algal growth — In transparent or semi-transparent tanks exposed to sunlight, algae proliferate on illuminated surfaces and in the water column, particularly during warm months.
  5. Chemical changes — pH may drift due to CO₂ absorption, pipe corrosion, or biological activity; dissolved solids may leach from accumulated debris.

3.2 The Detection Gap

Currently, tank owners detect water quality degradation through three unsatisfactory methods:

  1. Olfactory/gustatory detection — By the time the water smells "off" or tastes foul, significant microbial proliferation and/or biofilm shedding has already occurred. The consumer has already been exposed to degraded water.
  2. Visual detection — Discoloration or visible cloudiness indicates advanced contamination (turbidity typically exceeding 5 NTU), long after the degradation process began.
  3. Calendar-based cleaning — Tanks are cleaned on arbitrary schedules (monthly, quarterly, annually) regardless of actual contamination state, leading to either insufficient cleaning (health risk) or excessive cleaning (wasted water, labor, cost).

3.3 The Root Cause: Settled Sediment That No One Addresses

The fundamental physical reality that existing solutions fail to address is that the primary contamination reservoir in a stored-water tank is the settled sludge layer at the tank floor. This layer:

  • Accumulates continuously from incoming water particulates
  • Provides a nutrient-rich substrate for anaerobic bacterial growth
  • Re-suspends turbulently during inlet refilling, temporarily spiking water column turbidity
  • Is not removed by outlet draw-off (outlet is typically above the sludge layer)
  • Requires manual drain-and-scrub cleaning, which is labor-intensive, water-wasting, and infrequently performed

3.4 Limitations of Existing Approaches

Existing patents and products in this domain suffer from one or more of the following limitations:

  • Monitoring without actuation: Sensor-only systems (e.g., WO2024173389A1) detect water quality changes but provide no mechanism to remediate them; the user is merely alerted.
  • Full-tank drain cleaning: Automated cleaning systems (e.g., IN202441068267) use mechanical wipers and drain the entire tank, wasting large volumes of water and requiring the tank to be out of service.
  • Industrial-scale solutions: Sludge discharge systems (e.g., CN223988161U) are designed for large municipal sedimentation tanks using camera-based image analysis, not for domestic or institutional storage tanks.
  • In-pipe / point-of-use monitoring: Systems like WO2024173389A1 and Delta Faucet's application monitor water in pipes or at faucets, not in the storage tank where degradation actually occurs.
  • Chemical treatment approaches: Systems that dispense chemicals (e.g., chlorine) address microbial contamination but do not remove the physical sludge reservoir that sustains microbial growth.
  • Cloud dependence: Most IoT-based approaches require internet connectivity and cloud services, adding subscription cost and privacy concerns.

4. SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

4.1 Core Inventive Concept

The present invention provides a single integrated unit that combines continuous in-tank water quality sensing, composite contamination scoring with trend prediction, and a targeted bottom-flush actuation mechanism, all powered by an onboard solar-battery system, and operating entirely locally without cloud dependency.

The key inventive step is the closed-loop integration of sensing and targeted sediment-flush actuation:

  1. A multi-parameter sensor array submerged in the stored water continuously measures turbidity, pH, total dissolved solids (TDS), temperature, and water level.
  2. An onboard processor computes a composite TankScore — a single weighted, trend-aware index that quantifies the overall contamination state of the stored water, with particular emphasis on indicators of settled-sediment activity (turbidity trend, TDS leaching rate, refill-induced resuspension events).
  3. When the TankScore crosses a predetermined "clean-required" threshold (or when a manual trigger is received from a connected user interface), the processor actuates a normally-closed, bottom-mounted flush valve via a low-side or high-side switch, causing the valve to open for a predetermined duration.
  4. During the flush interval, the high-concentration sediment layer at the tank floor is selectively drained through the flush valve — without draining the bulk water column above. The valve is positioned at the lowest point of the tank to preferentially discharge the highest-turbidity, highest-TDS water.
  5. After the flush interval, the sensor array re-measures water quality and computes a post-flush TankScore, verifying the effectiveness of the flush. This closed-loop verification step is a critical aspect of the invention — the system confirms whether the flush actually improved water quality, and if not, may trigger additional flush cycles or escalate the alert to require manual cleaning.
  6. A complete event log (pre-flush score, flush duration, post-flush score, effectiveness ratio) is stored locally and presented on a user-accessible dashboard, enabling the tank owner to track long-term contamination trends and cleaning effectiveness.

5.4 How It Works — Operational Sequence

The invention operates as a continuous, autonomous sense–compute–actuate cycle. The following sequence describes one complete operating cycle of a working embodiment:

STEP 1 — WAKE & POWER-UP. The ESP32 wakes from deep sleep (default: every 3 hours) via RTC timer. It energises the 5 V sensor rail through a load switch, initialises the I²C bus (ADS1115), 1-Wire bus (DS18B20) and UART/GPIO for the level sensor. Deep-sleep current between cycles is <1 mA to conserve battery.
STEP 2 — ACQUIRE. All five sensors are read in sequence: turbidity (analog), TDS (analog), pH (via ADS1115 16-bit ADC), temperature (1-Wire) and level (ultrasonic time-of-flight). Readings are repeated 8–16 times and median-filtered to reject outliers. A timestamp is attached to the sample.
STEP 3 — FUSE & SCORE (edge computation). The processor normalises each parameter into a 0–100 sub-score, applies temperature compensation, then computes the weighted composite TankScore (turbidity 40% + TDS 25% + pH 20% + level 10% + temperature 5%). The sample is appended to a ring buffer in flash (last 112 samples ≈ 14 days).
STEP 4 — TREND & EVENT ANALYSIS. Linear regression is run over the 14-day window for turbidity, TDS and pH. The system classifies the current event as (i) gradual degradation, (ii) source-water contamination (level rise + turbidity spike), or (iii) sludge resuspension (turbidity spike with no level change). This classification determines the score penalty and the recommended action.
STEP 5 — DECIDE. The TankScore is mapped to a state: GOOD (80–100), WATCH (50–79), FLUSH RECOMMENDED (30–49) or CLEAN REQUIRED (0–29). In autonomous mode, CLEAN REQUIRED directly issues a flush command; in advisory mode, the request is pushed to the dashboard for user confirmation. A manual "Flush Now" button on the dashboard can also issue the command at any time.
STEP 6 — ACTUATE (flush). The GPIO drives the MOSFET gate, energising the solenoid valve. The valve opens for a computed duration (30–120 s, derived from tank volume, pre-flush turbidity and configured sludge thickness). Because the valve sits at the tank's lowest drain point, the dense, sediment-rich bottom layer discharges first — typically only 5–15% of tank volume — while the bulk water column above remains in the tank.
STEP 7 — CLOSE & SETTLE. The valve is de-energised (returns to normally-closed state). The system waits a settling interval (15–60 minutes) to allow residual suspended particles to redistribute before re-measuring.
STEP 8 — VERIFY (closed loop). All sensors are re-read and a post-flush TankScore is computed. Flush Effectiveness = (Pre-flush turbidity − Post-flush turbidity) / Pre-flush turbidity × 100. If effectiveness ≥ threshold (default 40%), the flush is logged as successful. If not, the system issues a second flush command or escalates to a persistent manual-cleaning-required alert. This verification step is the core novel feedback loop.
STEP 9 — REPORT & LOG. The full event record (timestamp, pre-flush score, flush duration, post-flush score, effectiveness ratio) is appended to flash storage. The embedded web server updates the dashboard with current readings, the TankScore banner, trend charts and the flush history. Battery/solar status is included.
STEP 10 — SLEEP. The sensor rail is de-energised, the ESP32 enters deep sleep, and the cycle repeats at the next timer interval.

5.5 IoT Subsystem (Highlighted)

The IoT subsystem is what converts a set of sensors and a valve into an autonomous, remotely observable, edge-intelligent system. It comprises the following elements:

(a) Edge Intelligence — Computation On-Device, Not in the Cloud

All decision-making runs on the ESP32's dual-core Xtensa processor: sensor fusion, weighted scoring, 14-day linear-regression trend analysis, three-way event classification, flush decision logic and post-flush verification. Nothing is sent to a cloud server for analysis. This means:

  • Flush decisions are made in real time on-device, immune to internet outages or server downtime.
  • No user data leaves the premises — an architectural privacy guarantee.
  • No recurring cloud cost, no account, no API key, no subscription.
  • The device remains fully functional with zero internet connectivity.

(b) Local Wireless Communication & Dashboard

The ESP32 operates as a Wi-Fi access point (or joins an existing LAN in station mode) and hosts an embedded HTTP server. Any browser-equipped device on the same network — phone, tablet, laptop — can open a URL (resolved via mDNS, e.g. tankguard.local) and load the dashboard. The dashboard provides:

  • Real-time gauges for turbidity, TDS, pH, temperature and level
  • Colour-coded TankScore status banner (GOOD / WATCH / FLUSH RECOMMENDED / CLEAN REQUIRED)
  • Trend charts at 24-hour, 7-day, 30-day and 90-day horizons
  • Manual "Flush Now" override and "Tank Cleaned" confirmation buttons
  • Flush history log with pre/post scores and effectiveness percentages
  • Battery and solar charging status; sensor calibration interface

Because the dashboard is served from the device itself, it works in buildings with no internet, in rural installations, and in situations where the owner does not want water-usage data held by a third-party cloud provider.

(c) Data Persistence at the Edge

All sensor samples and flush event records are written to the ESP32's flash filesystem (LittleFS/SPIFFS) using wear-levelled append operations. The 14-day ring buffer supports trend regression; longer history supports the 90-day charts. Data survives power cycles and reboots. Optional export is available via a CSV download endpoint on the local server.

(d) Optional Extended IoT Connectivity (Claimed as Preferred Embodiment, Not Required)

In a preferred optional embodiment, the same ESP32 can additionally connect to the internet and push alerts through one or more channels — MQTT to a self-hosted broker, e-mail, SMS gateway, or a messaging-app bot — without changing any core logic, because the flush decision is already made at the edge. The internet link is used only for notification and remote status viewing, never for control. This keeps the system's safety-critical function independent of connectivity.

(e) OTA Firmware Updates

An optional over-the-air firmware update mechanism allows the device's firmware to be improved after deployment (e.g. new TankScore weights, new sensor drivers) while the unit remains installed inside the tank — no physical removal required.

(f) Multi-Tank Fleet Management (Optional Embodiment)

Where multiple tanks exist on one site (apartment complex, school, hospital), each TankGuard unit advertises its own mDNS hostname and can be aggregated by a simple local dashboard that polls each device's HTTP endpoint — a lightweight local "fleet" view with no cloud platform involved.

5.6 TankScore Computation Engine

The TankScore is a composite numerical index (0–100) computed by the onboard processor. It integrates three classes of information:

(a) Instantaneous Parameter Sub-Scores

Each sensor parameter is converted to a 0–100 sub-score based on its distance from a safe reference band. For example, turbidity below 1 NTU scores 100; between 1–5 NTU scores linearly from 100 to 50; above 5 NTU scores 0.

(b) Trend Analysis

Linear regression is performed on the last 14 days (112 readings at 3-hour intervals) of turbidity, TDS, and pH data. The slope (rate of change) of each parameter contributes a trend bonus or penalty to the TankScore. A rising turbidity trend triggers a premature WATCH or CLEAN-NOW status even before the absolute threshold is breached, providing predictive capability.

(c) Event Classification

The system classifies water quality events into three categories based on correlated multi-parameter signatures:

  • Gradual degradation: Turbidity slowly rising with stable pH → biofilm accumulation. TankScore penalized proportionally to slope.
  • Source water contamination: Level rise (refill detected) followed by turbidity spike → contaminated incoming water. TankScore penalized based on peak turbidity but trend analysis is suppressed for settling period.
  • Sludge resuspension: Turbidity spike without level change (inlet turbulence, temperature convection) → resuspension of existing bottom layer. TankScore penalized and flush recommendation issued (this is the primary flush trigger).

5.7 Automated Sediment-Flush Actuation Subsystem

The flush subsystem comprises:

  1. Flush valve: A normally-closed, electrically actuated valve (solenoid or motorized ball valve) with a bore diameter selected for the tank size (typically ½ inch to 1 inch for domestic tanks). The valve is installed at the lowest drain point of the tank via a T-fitting or existing drain tap. The valve body material is selected for potable water contact (brass with food-grade EPDM seal, or SS316).
  2. Valve driver: A MOSFET-based low-side switch driven by a GPIO output of the processor, with flyback protection (diode or RC snubber) for inductive loads (solenoid coils). The driver circuit is housed in the main enclosure and connected to the valve by a waterproof cable.
  3. Flush duration controller: The processor controls the valve open duration based on (a) tank volume (derived from level sensor), (b) pre-flush turbidity level, and (c) user-configurable sludge thickness parameter. Typical flush durations range from 30 seconds to 120 seconds for domestic tanks.
  4. Post-flush verification: After the flush interval, the system waits a predetermined settling time (typically 15–60 minutes), re-measures all sensor parameters, and computes the post-flush TankScore. The flush effectiveness is calculated as:

Flush Effectiveness (%) = (Pre-flush Turbidity − Post-flush Turbidity) / Pre-flush Turbidity × 100

If effectiveness exceeds a predetermined threshold (e.g., 40%), the flush is confirmed successful and the TankScore is updated. If effectiveness is below threshold, the system may issue a second flush command or escalate to a manual-cleaning-required alert.

5.8 Power Management Subsystem

The unit is self-powered by a solar photovoltaic panel and rechargeable battery system:

  • Solar panel: 5–10 W, 12 V nominal, mounted adjacent to or integral with the enclosure.
  • Charge controller: MPPT or PWM, with overcharge and over-discharge protection.
  • Battery: Lithium-ion (18650 or LiFePO₄) pack, capacity selected for 2–3 weeks of zero-sun autonomy.
  • Regulated power rails: 12 V bus for flush valve actuation; 5 V rail for sensor excitation; 3.3 V rail for processor and digital circuits.
  • Duty cycling: The processor enters deep-sleep mode between measurement cycles, waking at predetermined intervals (default: every 3 hours) to power the sensor rail, perform measurements, compute TankScore, evaluate flush need, and serve any pending user requests. Deep-sleep current is minimized (<1 mA) to maximize battery life.

5.9 User Interface Subsystem

The processor hosts a local web server accessible over the tank's WiFi network (no internet required). The interface provides:

  • Real-time gauge display of all measured parameters
  • TankScore status banner with color-coded indication
  • Trend charts (24-hour, 7-day, 30-day, 90-day) for each parameter
  • "Flush Now" button for manual override of automated flush
  • "Tank Cleaned" confirmation button (resets the cleaning interval counter and logs the event)
  • Flush history log (timestamp, pre-flush score, post-flush score, effectiveness)
  • Battery and solar charging status
  • Calibration interface for sensor maintenance

9. PATENTABILITY ASSESSMENT

Novelty (Section 32 of Indian Patents Act, 1970): The claimed subject matter is not anticipated by any single prior art reference found in the preliminary search. The combination of in-tank multi-parameter sensing, trend-predictive composite scoring, selective bottom-flush actuation, and closed-loop flush verification in a single self-powered unit is not disclosed in any surveyed reference.

Inventive Step / Non-Obviousness: While each individual component (ESP32, sensors, solenoid valve, MOSFET driver, solar panel, embedded web server) is known in isolation, their specific combination and integration into a closed-loop edge-IoT system that (a) distinguishes settled-sediment contamination from other contamination sources at the network edge, (b) triggers a selective partial flush (not full drain), and (c) verifies flush effectiveness by re-measurement, is not obvious to a person skilled in the art of water tank maintenance or IoT sensor systems. The closed-loop verification step in particular represents a non-trivial technical contribution that addresses a real problem (silent flush failure due to valve clogging or insufficient duration) that is not recognised in prior art; and the decision to execute the entire control loop on-device while serving the dashboard over the local network only — eliminating the cloud dependency universal in prior-art IoT water monitors — is an architecturally significant and non-obvious design choice.

Industrial Applicability: The invention is directly applicable to millions of domestic and institutional water storage tanks in India and similar regions. The system uses commercially available components and can be manufactured at low cost. The self-powered, cloud-free design makes it suitable for deployment in areas with unreliable electricity and internet connectivity.

10. RECOMMENDED CLAIM STRATEGY

  1. Independent System Claim (Claim 1): Broadest claim covering the complete integrated system. Drafted above.
  2. Dependent Claim — Closed-Loop Verification: The system of Claim 1, wherein the processor is configured to compute a flush effectiveness metric by comparing pre-flush and post-flush composite scores, and to issue a secondary flush command or a manual-cleaning alert when the flush effectiveness metric is below a predetermined threshold.
  3. Dependent Claim — Trend-Predictive Triggering: The system of Claim 1, wherein the composite score incorporates a trend component derived from linear regression analysis of historical turbidity and TDS readings over a period of 7 to 30 days, and wherein the flush actuation signal is generated when the trend component indicates a rate of degradation that would cause the instantaneous parameters to exceed safe thresholds within a predetermined number of days, even if the instantaneous parameters have not yet exceeded said thresholds.
  4. Dependent Claim — Event Classification: The system of Claim 1, wherein the processor classifies water quality events into a plurality of event types based on correlated multi-parameter signatures, said event types comprising at least: gradual degradation, source-water contamination, and sludge resuspension, and wherein each event type produces a different TankScore contribution and a different recommended remediation action.
  5. Dependent Claim — Selective Partial Flush: The system of Claim 1, wherein the flush valve open duration is determined based on at least one of: tank volume, pre-flush turbidity level, and a user-configurable sludge thickness parameter, and wherein said open duration is selected to discharge no more than 15% of total tank volume.
  6. Dependent Claim — Edge/IoT Architecture: The system of Claim 1, wherein all scoring, trend analysis, event classification and flush decision logic execute locally on the on-device processor, with the full control loop operating without internet, cloud, subscription or account, and the local wireless interface being the sole user-facing data path. (Claim 7 above.)
  7. Dependent Claim — Duty-Cycled Edge Sensing: The system of Claim 1, wherein the processor sleeps between measurement cycles and wakes on timer to energise a switched sensor rail, acquire, compute, decide and serve requests, enabling multi-week solar autonomy. (Claim 8 above.)
  8. Method Claim: A method of maintaining water quality in a storage tank using an IoT edge device, comprising: waking from low-power sleep; energising a sensor power rail; continuously measuring turbidity, TDS, pH, temperature, and water level in stored water; computing a composite water quality score with trend analysis at the edge; classifying the score into quality states; actuating a bottom-mounted flush valve when the score indicates sediment removal is needed; re-measuring water quality after flush completion; computing a flush effectiveness metric; logging the effectiveness metric locally on the device; serving the results on a local-network dashboard; and returning to sleep.
