1. Abstract

Rooftop and ground-level water storage tanks are the most neglected link in the household drinking-water chain. Although the water entering them from the municipal supply is treated and disinfected, the water that sits in a tank degrades over time: the chlorine residual dissipates, sediment settles on the floor, biofilm grows on the walls, and microbial populations rise—so the water coming out of the tap is frequently very different from the water that came in. Because tanks are often forgotten, this degradation is discovered only after it affects health or causes foul odour and taste.

This proposal presents TankGuard, an IoT-enabled, solar-powered system that continuously measures the key water-quality parameters of water stored in a household tank—pH, turbidity, total dissolved solids (TDS) and temperature, together with water level—and computes a single TankScore that answers one question conclusively: “is it time to clean this tank?” The device is wall/lid mounted, powered by a solar panel and Li-ion battery, stores ninety days of history locally, and exposes a phone-friendly web dashboard over the household WiFi network with no cloud service required. The prototype targets a single household tank from low-cost, off-the-shelf analogue sensor modules within a tight budget, with a design that scales to multiple tanks and a custom PCB in a later stage.

Keywords: IoT, water quality, ESP32, open-source hardware, smart home, turbidity sensor, solar power, sustainable development.

2. Introduction and Motivation

Safe drinking water is a fundamental requirement for public health, but its safety is not guaranteed solely at the treatment plant. In urban and semi-urban homes, water is typically stored in overhead or underground tanks that are topped up irregularly from municipal lines or borewells. WHO identifies storage as a critical recontamination point: "the safe water chain breaks at the point of storage" unless tanks are kept clean and covered [1][2]. Practically, household tanks are opened rarely—cleanings happen on the order of months to years—and by the time the water smells or tastes bad, the tank has already become a growth reservoir.

The motivation for this project is straightforward: tank cleaning is forgotten because there is no cheap, automatic way to know when cleaning is due. Current practice is calendar-based guesswork ("clean every N months") or reactive (clean only after the water becomes unusable). A low-cost sensor node that monitors the actual chemical and biological state of the stored water converts guesswork into evidence, and can alert the owner precisely when the tank needs attention—so that cleaning effort is spent exactly when it is needed.

TankGuard is designed around three principles:

  • In-tank, not in-pipe: the sensors sit in the stored water, because the problem to measure is what happens while water is stored.
  • Local and private: all data, history and alerts live on the device and are served on the home WiFi—no cloud account, no subscription, no data leaving the home.
  • Self-powered and low-maintenance: solar panel + battery, deep-sleep power management, cost-effective calibrated analogue probes, and a cleaning recommendation engine.

3. Problem Statement

Household water storage tanks are rarely cleaned in time because there is no practical, affordable and automatic indicator of when cleaning is required. As a result:

  • Stored water loses disinfectant residual and becomes biologically re-contaminated during storage.
  • Sediment and sludge accumulate on the tank floor; biofilm forms on submerged surfaces.
  • Consumers detect the problem only through odour, taste, or visible discolouration—after contamination has already occurred.
  • Manual cleaning is irregular, inconsistent, and wastes large volumes of water because it cannot be scheduled against actual tank condition.

Cleaning is a manual, labour-intensive chore with no widely available, safe way to carry it out automatically once the need is detected. This project therefore focuses on the first, decisive problem: bringing evidence to the decision. (P1) continuous, evidence-based detection of "tank needs cleaning".

4. Objectives

  1. Design a solar-powered, wall/lid-mounted IoT node based on an ESP32 that measures pH, turbidity, TDS, temperature and water level of stored tank water.
  2. Develop a reproducible calibration procedure for each sensor so that readings remain accurate over months of continuous submersion.
  3. Implement a deep-sleep based power-management scheme that allows the node to run indefinitely from a solar panel and a Li-ion battery.
  4. Design the TankScore decision engine—a weighted, trend-aware indicator that classifies tank state as GOOD, WATCH or CLEAN-REQUIRED, and tracks days since the last confirmed cleaning.
  5. Build a local, phone-accessible web dashboard (no cloud) showing live readings, history graphs, status and a "Tank cleaned" confirmation control.
  6. Validate the complete system on a real household tank through a multi-week soak test, and document calibration drift and maintenance.

5. Literature Review

IoT water-quality monitoring is an active research area. Sharma et al. describe an ESP32-based system integrating pH, turbidity, TDS and temperature sensors with on-device machine learning and cloud integration, reporting stable readings over 24-hour operation and low repeatability error for pH; notably, the sensing array is assembled from discrete analog probes [3]. The work reported in the International Journal of Computer Science and Publication demonstrates an ESP32 + Adafruit.IO design measuring the same four parameters, and provides a useful calibration methodology with buffer solutions (pH 4.0, 7.0, 10.0) and standard TDS/turbidity samples [4]. Chavhan et al. extend this to industrial wastewater with a multi-parameter suite including dissolved oxygen, electrical conductivity and water level, validated against laboratory instruments [5]. The HydroSense framework proposes a dual-microcontroller topology—an Arduino dedicated to precision analog capture (pH, DO) and an ESP32 handling connectivity—with dissolved oxygen probes of the galvanic type that require no polarisation time [6].

First, most systems transmit to public clouds and offer no local storage or offline dashboard. Second, the analogue probe modules that keep a prototype cheap drift and benefit from regular recalibration; production hardening would migrate to industrial RS485/Modbus probes with automatic temperature compensation and IP68 enclosures [7]. Third, none of the surveyed systems addresses the cleaning decision itself—they report quality values but not an actionable "clean now" trigger. This proposal directly targets those gaps: an in-tank ESP32 node built from low-cost analogue sensor modules, with a local web dashboard and a decision engine that turns continuous readings into a single, conclusive cleaning verdict.

Several open-source projects already exist online and validate the ESP32 + pH/TDS/turbidity approach this proposal adopts: water-quality-monitor (ESP32 with pH, EC, TDS, turbidity and temperature feeding a Blynk dashboard) [11], an end-to-end ESP32 + Python web-dashboard system [12], a ThingSpeak-based TDS/turbidity monitor with SMS alerts built for under ₹2,000 [13], a tank-specific water-level + pH + turbidity monitor with pump control [14], and a cloud (AWS)-based monitor with ML-gated alerts [15]. None of them couples solar autonomy with a fully local dashboard and a cleaning-decision engine, which is exactly the gap TankGuard fills.

6. Scope and Justification

The project scope is a single prototype system for one household tank, built first from off-the-shelf modules on a breadboard to validate sensing and firmware, with a later custom PCB and IP65 enclosure. The design deliberately chooses:

  • Low-cost analogue sensor modules (Gravity/Dual-head-class) in place of industrial RS485/Modbus probes: they are a fraction of the price, breadboard-friendly, and accurate enough for a validated prototype when calibrated against handheld meters. Drift is handled by a quarterly recalibration procedure, and production hardening can drop in industrial probes later.
  • Local-only connectivity. Data never leaves the home; the ESP32 serves a web app over WiFi (mDNS hostname). This avoids recurring costs, privacy concerns and dependence on a commercial cloud.
  • Single-node architecture. One device measures, decides, stores and serves the dashboard; no separate actuator module is within this project’s scope, though the decision output is designed to drive one in a future extension.

7.1 Modular System Product Line

TankGuard is architected as a two-module product family around a single base unit, so each household buys only the features it actually needs: the base unit (M1) monitors water quality, and one optional plug-in module (M2) adds safe pump control. The customer selects the combination at purchase.

Module options for the TankGuard product line (customer-selectable).

M2 — Auto-Off / Auto-On Pump ModuleOpto-relay + motor-rated contactor with local TFT and one-button override; cuts the pump when the tank is full (level trigger) or water quality is unsafe, restarts on demand, and protects the motor from dry-running.Wanted: pump automation driven by the same monitoring verdict.

Module M2 is outside the tank-side prototype’s build scope (the prototype covers the M1 base only, preserving the single-node guarantee in Section 6). The base still provides the decision signals for it today: the TankScore, level and alert triggers are exposed on an expansion header and emitted as dashboard/POST events, so M2 can be added later by plugging in the home board and enabling the module in the configuration file—no sensor changes required.

7.2 Monitoring Node

The node is a wall/lid-mounted waterproof, heat-protected enclosure (IP66 box with a foil-foam thermal liner and a reflective heat shield for exposed rooftop mounting in Indian sun) containing the ESP32, the ADS1115 ADC, power electronics, and a humidity-sealed cable gland through which the probe cables pass to the water. A full table of sensor choices and their rationale is given in Table 1. All three primary liquid sensors are low-cost analogue modules: pH, TDS/EC and turbidity are read as small analogue voltages by their interface boards and digitised by a dedicated ADS1115 16-bit ADC, which keeps them well clear of the ESP32’s native ADC nonlinearity. This is the key prototype trade-off—analogue modules cost a fraction of industrial RS485 probes, and accuracy is secured by a reproducible calibration procedure validated against a handheld meter during bring-up.

pHAnalog glass electrode (SEN0161/Gravity), via ADC6.5–8.5Drift signals algae, decay, seepage or corrosion.
TDS / ECAnalog conductivity (SEN0244/Gravity), via ADC< 500 ppmDissolved solids leach from settled debris and scale.

7.3 The "When to Clean" Quality Engine (TankScore)

The decision engine converts raw readings into one actionable status. It combines two signals:

  1. Interval counter: days since the last confirmed cleaning (set via the dashboard) versus a configurable interval (default 90 days, adjustable 30–180). Expiry alone raises a WATCH flag.
  2. Water-quality score (0–100): each parameter contributes a sub-score based on how far the reading is from its safe band (Table 2), weighted by importance. A 14-day slope of turbidity, TDS and pH detects deterioration in progress even before a hard threshold is breached (e.g. turbidity climbing steadily but still under 1 NTU).
Table 2 — Quality thresholds driving the TankScore.
ParameterGOODWATCHCLEAN-REQUIRED flag
Turbidity (NTU)< 11–5> 5
TDS (ppm)< 300300–500> 500
pH6.5–8.56.0–6.5 / 8.5–9.0< 6.0 or > 9.0
Water temperatureNot flagged directly; warm water (> 30 °C) raises the risk weight of the biological indicators.

The weighted result is a single status: GOOD (no action), WATCH (deteriorating or near thresholds), or CLEAN-REQUIRED (recommends that the tank be cleaned). The status is displayed prominently on the dashboard. When CLEAN-REQUIRED is reached, a persistent alert is raised; the dashboard’s one-tap "Tank cleaned" control logs the completed cleaning and restarts the interval counter, so the next "due" alert begins a fresh period.

7.4 Local Data and Web Dashboard

All history is stored on the ESP32 in its flash filesystem (approximately 90 days at a 3-hour sampling interval in a ring buffer). The ESP32 serves a single-page web application—gauge cards per parameter, history charts, battery/solar status, the TankScore banner, a "Tank cleaned ✓" button, and calibration screens. The phone simply opens http://tankmon.local when on the same WiFi network. On first boot, the device opens a captive-portal access point so the owner can enter WiFi credentials once. No cloud service, account, or subscription is required.

7.5 Power and Energy Management

The 12 V solar bus powers the sensor probes; a buck converter and a low-quiescent LDO feed a clean 3.3 V rail for the ESP32. To keep average consumption low, the node spends most of its time in deep sleep (timer wake), waking every 3 hours to power the sensor rail, let probes settle, sample, store, and publish over WiFi, then sleeping again. The RTC and a battery-monitor ADC stay alive. This measure–sleep–publish loop is the enabler for a fully solar-autonomous node (Section 11 gives the numbers).

9. Software and Firmware Architecture

The firmware is written for the Arduino core under PlatformIO (ESP32), organised in modules so each sensor and service can be developed and tested independently:

  main:  measure → compute TankScore → store → serve → deep sleep
  ├── sensors/    ph, tds, turbidity, temp, level, battery drivers
  ├── power/      battery ADC, sensor-rail enable, deep-sleep scheduler
  ├── wifi/       station + mDNS + captive-portal first-boot provisioning
  ├── server/     HTTP REST + WebSocket + LittleFS static web app
  ├── storage/    LittleFS config, calibration, 90-day history ring buffer
  └── quality/    TankScore engine, trend detection, alert rules

Key engineering details:

  • Noise rejection: the three analogue probes are sampled as ∼100 samples each through the ADS1115, sorted, and the median used; pH and TDS are temperature-compensated in software against the DS18B20.
  • Calibration: pH via two/three-point buffer calibration (4.0/6.86/9.18), turbidity via 0 NTU and 1000 NTU formazin standards, TDS via standard conductivity solutions; offsets are persisted in a calibration file and re-applied on every boot. A maintenance reminder re-runs calibration every 3 months.
  • Time: NTP synchronisation whenever WiFi is online; the RTC keeps time in deep sleep.
  • Analogue channels: pH, TDS and turbidity are sampled through the ADS1115 to bypass the ESP32’s native ADC nonlinearity; like every channel, they are median-filtered and temperature-compensated, and gated by the sensor-rail power switch so they draw current only during the measurement window.

11. Power Budget and Solar Sizing

Energy autonomy is the most important reliability requirement. Table 8 summarises the projected consumption of the measure–sleep–publish loop.

Table 8 — Power budget (Monitoring Node).
StateCurrent (avg)Duty / dayCharge used / day
Deep sleep (ESP32 + RTC only, rail off)~0.1 mA22 h2.2 mAh
Measure window (analogue probes settle, ADS1115 sampling)~90 mA1.33 h (4 min × 8 wake-ups)120 mAh
WiFi publish + NTP~250 mA1.5 h (approx. 12 min/wake-up)375 mAh
Total average daily consumption~500 mAh @ 12 V

With a 4×18650 pack (~10.8 Ah usable at 12 V), the node has roughly 3 weeks of autonomy with zero sun—far exceeding the worst realistic cloud cover. A 10 W panel yields ~50 Wh/day at 5 peak-sun-hours, comfortably recharging a 6 Wh/day draw even in winter. The analogue probe boards (pH, TDS, turbidity) draw only a few mA each and are switched with the rest of the sensor rail, so the daily budget is essentially unchanged.

12. Expected Outcomes

  • A working, solar-powered prototype Monitoring Node producing calibrated readings for pH, turbidity, TDS, temperature and water level, with ≤3-minute settle time per measurement window.
  • A base module exposing TankScore and level triggers on an expansion header so the optional pump-control (M2) module bolts on without sensor changes.
  • A validated TankScore engine that correctly identifies GOOD / WATCH / CLEAN-REQUIRED states on a real tank, including trend-based early warnings.
  • A local web dashboard (no cloud) with live gauges, 90-day history graphs, battery/solar telemetry, and one-tap "Tank cleaned" confirmation.
  • Documented calibration procedure, probe-drift measurements from a multi-week soak test, and a maintenance guide (membrane/KCl care, quarterly recalibration).

15. Conclusion

TankGuard addresses a real, neglected problem—the silent degradation of household stored water—with a practical, evidence-based, fully local and solar-autonomous system. By measuring the parameters that actually change while water sits in a tank and computing a single TankScore, plus tracking how long it has been since a confirmed cleaning, the device moves tank maintenance from guesswork to a clear, timely schedule. The project follows a measured path—validated sensor bring-up, robust firmware, local dashboard, then real-tank soak test—producing a documented, reproducible prototype whose design can later be hardened into a custom PCB and sealed product.
